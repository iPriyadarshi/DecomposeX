Theory

What LDU factorization actually says

Three definitions, two theorems and one honest caveat. By the end of this page you will know exactly which matrices factor, which do not, and why the factors are unique when they exist.

Why split a matrix at all?

Solving Ax = b by elimination costs roughly n³/3 operations. If you have a second right-hand side, naive elimination makes you pay that cost all over again, even though the work depended only on A, not on b.

A factorization records that work once. Elimination is fundamentally a sequence of row operations, and every such sequence can be bottled up as a matrix product. Once you have A = LDU, solving a new system costs only n², and the same three factors also hand you the determinant, the rank, the inverse and a definiteness test.

The point of a factorization is not the factors. It is that triangular systems are easy, and a factorization is how you turn a hard system into a chain of easy ones.

Why triangular systems are easy

In a lower-triangular system the first equation involves only x1, so you read it off immediately; the second then involves only x1 and x2, and so on down the matrix. This is forward substitution. Running it upwards through an upper-triangular system is back substitution. Each costs about n²/2 multiply–add pairs, and if the diagonal is all 1s it needs no division at all.

The three kinds of factor

Definition: triangular and unit triangular

A square matrix L is lower triangular if ℓij = 0 whenever i < j: everything strictly above the diagonal vanishes. It is unit lower triangular if in addition every diagonal entry is 1. Upper triangular and unit upper triangular are defined the same way with the inequality reversed.

A matrix that is both lower and upper triangular is diagonal. This tiny observation is the whole engine of the uniqueness proof below.

Definition: LDU factorization

An LDU factorization of a square matrix A is a product

A = L D U

in which

  • L is unit lower triangular: the multipliers, with 1s on the diagonal;
  • D is diagonal: the pivots d1, …, dn;
  • U is unit upper triangular: the shape of the reduced system, with 1s on the diagonal.

The factorization is called non-singular when every dk ≠ 0, which is exactly when A is invertible.

Fixing 1s on both unit diagonals is what makes the factorization canonical. Without that convention you could slide any non-zero scale factor from L into D and out into U, and the factors would not be unique.

LDU compared with LU

An LU factorization asks only for A = LÛ with one triangular factor on each side. That leaves a choice of normalisation, and different textbooks make it differently:

NameNormalisationRelation to LDU
Doolittle L has 1s on the diagonal Û = D·U: the pivots stay in the upper factor
Crout U has 1s on the diagonal L̂ = L·D: the pivots stay in the lower factor
LDU both have 1s on the diagonal the pivots get a factor of their own

So LDU is not a different computation from LU; it is the same elimination with the scaling made explicit. That is why the calculator on this site reports both: elimination naturally produces Û, and dividing each row of Û by its own diagonal entry separates D from U.

When does a factorization exist?

Not every matrix has one. The obstruction is always the same: elimination needs to divide by a pivot, and a pivot can be zero. The remarkable thing is that you can predict this in advance, without running the elimination, by looking at determinants of the top-left corners.

Definition: leading principal minor

The k-th leading principal minor of A, written Dk, is the determinant of the top-left k × k block of A. By convention D0 = 1, and Dn = det A.

Theorem 1: existence and the minor test

Let A be an n × n matrix over a field. Then A has a non-singular LDU factorization; that is, A = LDU with unit triangular L, U and invertible diagonal D, if and only if

D1 ≠ 0,   D2 ≠ 0,   …,   Dn ≠ 0.

In that case the pivots are determined by the minors:

dk = Dk ⁄ Dk−1,    k = 1, …, n.
Proof of the minor formula

Suppose A = LDU. Because all three factors are triangular, the top-left k × k block of the product is the product of the top-left k × k blocks, entries from outside the corner cannot reach into it. Writing Ak for that block,

Ak = Lk Dk Uk.

Taking determinants and using det Lk = det Uk = 1 (triangular with 1s on the diagonal) gives Dk = d1d2⋯dk. Dividing consecutive cases yields dk = Dk/Dk−1, and since each dk ≠ 0, every Dk ≠ 0. Conversely, if every minor is non-zero then no pivot encountered during elimination can vanish, because the pivot at stage k equals Dk/Dk−1, so the elimination runs to completion and produces the factors.

Two consequences worth keeping in mind. First, the test is about corners, not about individual entries: a matrix can have a perfectly innocent-looking a22 and still fail, because the pivot at stage 2 is what is left of a22 after stage 1.

What about singular matrices?

Theorem 1 is about invertible D. If we relax that and allow zeros on the diagonal, some singular matrices still factor and some genuinely do not. The dividing line is easy to state once you notice that dk = 0 forces row k of DU to be entirely zero, whatever U holds:

  • If elimination leaves row k completely empty whenever dk = 0, the identity still holds. The factorization exists but is no longer unique, that row of U can be anything unit triangular.
  • If a zero pivot sits beside a non-empty row, the two sides cannot be reconciled and no LDU factorization exists: not even a singular one. The LU form A = LÛ survives, which is why the calculator still shows it.

Uniqueness

When a non-singular LDU factorization exists, there is exactly one. The proof is short and worth reading, because it explains why the unit diagonals are not an arbitrary convention.

Lemma

Unit lower-triangular matrices form a group: a product of two is unit lower triangular, and so is the inverse of one. The same holds for unit upper-triangular matrices.

Theorem 2: uniqueness

If A is invertible and A = L1D1U1 = L2D2U2 are two non-singular LDU factorizations, then L1 = L2, D1 = D2 and U1 = U2.

Proof

From L1D1U1 = L2D2U2, multiply on the left by L2−1 and on the right by U1−1:

(L2−1L1) D1 = D2 (U2U1−1).

By the lemma, L2−1L1 is unit lower triangular, so the left side is lower triangular with diagonal exactly D1. Likewise U2U1−1 is unit upper triangular, so the right side is upper triangular with diagonal exactly D2. A matrix that is simultaneously lower and upper triangular is diagonal; therefore both sides equal their own diagonal, giving D1 = D2. Cancelling the invertible D1 leaves L2−1L1 = I and U2U1−1 = I.

Notice exactly where invertibility of D is used, in the cancellation. That is precisely why the singular case above loses uniqueness.

Permutations: PA = LDU

A zero pivot is not fatal, because the rows of a matrix may be reordered freely. Reordering is itself a matrix operation: swapping rows i and j of A is the same as forming E·A, where E is the identity with those two rows swapped. A product of such swaps is a permutation matrix P.

Theorem 3: factorization with pivoting

For every invertible A there is a permutation matrix P such that

PA = LDU

with L unit lower triangular, D diagonal and invertible, and U unit upper triangular.

The reason is simply that elimination can always find a pivot: if A is invertible, no column can be entirely zero below the diagonal at the stage it is reached, since that would make the columns linearly dependent. Choose any non-zero entry, swap it up, and continue.

Careful

P multiplies on the left, so it permutes rows. When you use the factorization to solve Ax = b, the right-hand side must be permuted the same way: LDUx = Pb, not b. Forgetting this is the single most common error when implementing pivoted elimination.

Since P is a product of s row swaps, det P = (−1)s, and taking determinants of PA = LDU gives a formula the calculator uses:

det A = (−1)s · d1d2⋯dn

The symmetric case: A = LDLᵀ

Symmetry cuts the work in half, and uniqueness is what proves it.

Theorem 4: LDLᵀ

If A is symmetric and invertible with all leading principal minors non-zero, then its LDU factorization satisfies U = Lᵀ, so

A = L D Lᵀ.
Proof

Write A = LDU. Transposing, Aᵀ = Uᵀ Dᵀ Lᵀ = Uᵀ D Lᵀ, because a diagonal matrix is its own transpose. Now Uᵀ is unit lower triangular and Lᵀ is unit upper triangular, so this is also a non-singular LDU factorization, of Aᵀ. But A = Aᵀ, so both are factorizations of the same matrix, and Theorem 2 forces them to coincide: L = Uᵀ, hence U = Lᵀ.

Positive definiteness and Cholesky

For symmetric matrices the pivots carry a further meaning.

Theorem 5: Sylvester's criterion

A symmetric matrix A is positive definite, meaning xᵀAx > 0 for every non-zero x, if and only if every leading principal minor is positive, equivalently if and only if every pivot dk in its LDLᵀ factorization is positive.

The equivalence of the two forms is immediate from Dk = d1⋯dk: all the minors are positive exactly when all the pivots are. And when they are positive, each has a real square root, so D = D1/2D1/2 and

A = LDLᵀ = (LD1/2)(LD1/2)ᵀ = RRᵀ,

which is the Cholesky factorization. So Cholesky is not a separate algorithm so much as LDLᵀ with the diagonal absorbed, and LDLᵀ has the advantage of needing no square roots at all, which is why it is preferred when the matrix might be indefinite.

The family of factorizations

Where LDU sits among its relatives:

FactorizationFormRequiresCost
LU A = LÛ minors D1…Dn−1 ≠ 0 ~n³/3
LDU A = LDU same, plus det A ≠ 0 for invertible D ~n³/3
PLU / PLDU PA = LDU only that A is invertible ~n³/3
LDLᵀ A = LDLᵀ symmetric, minors non-zero ~n³/6
Cholesky A = RRᵀ symmetric positive definite ~n³/6
QR A = QR nothing, always exists ~2n³/3

Costs count multiply–add pairs for the leading term only. QR is twice the work but needs no pivoting for stability, which is the usual trade.

Summary

  • LDU writes A as unit-lower × diagonal × unit-upper. The unit diagonals are what make it canonical.
  • It exists with invertible D exactly when every leading principal minor is non-zero, and then the pivots are ratios of consecutive minors.
  • When it exists, it is unique. Invertibility of D is what makes the cancellation in the proof legal, so singular factorizations lose uniqueness.
  • A zero pivot is repaired by a row permutation: PA = LDU exists for every invertible matrix.
  • Some singular matrices have no LDU factorization at all: a zero pivot next to a non-empty row cannot be reconciled.
  • For symmetric A, uniqueness forces U = Lᵀ; positive pivots then mean positive definite, and Cholesky follows.
